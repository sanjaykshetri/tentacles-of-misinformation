8  What I Would Do Differently

9 Epilogue: What I Built, What I Learned, What Comes Next

This started as a Master’s thesis question: why do smart people believe false things?

It became a two-year journey through cognitive psychology, NLP, epidemiological modeling, and production engineering. By the end I had something I didn’t expect—not just a system that detects misinformation, but a clear-eyed view of how hard the problem really is, and a sharper sense of what it means to build things that matter.

This epilogue is my honest reflection on what worked, what failed, what I understand now that I didn’t at the start, and where I think this work goes next.

9.0.1 1. Start with behavioral science — not as an afterthought

The 4–5% accuracy gain from behavioral features sounds modest. But it was the hardest part to earn and the most intellectually interesting part of the project. Understanding why people fall for misinformation—emotional triggers, false certainty, artificial simplicity—changed how I looked at every downstream decision.

Next time, I’d make cognitive mechanisms the foundation, not the fusion layer. Before asking “what model should I use?”, I’d ask “what does the cognitive science tell me about what to measure?”

9.0.2 2. Baselines are undervalued — everywhere

The instinct is to jump to transformers. I felt it too.

But TF-IDF at 81.2% was, in many ways, the most valuable result in this project:

  • It proved the problem was learnable from text alone
  • It exposed a data leakage bug that DistilBERT silently absorbed
  • It runs on a laptop CPU in milliseconds
  • It tells you exactly which words matter — no black box

DistilBERT at 85.75% is impressive. But if I hadn’t built the baseline first, I wouldn’t have known what 4.5% accuracy actually cost.

9.0.3 3. Production thinking belongs at the start, not the end

I built the research system first and bolted on production thinking afterward. That was backwards.

If I’d known early that the deployment target was <50ms on CPU with explainability requirements, I would have made different model choices, built the monitoring infrastructure earlier, and spared myself a significant rewrite. Latency, fairness, and interpretability aren’t post-launch concerns — they’re design constraints.

9.0.4 4. Fairness is iterative, not a final audit

I measured fairness after the system was “done.” That’s the wrong frame. Every model update changes the fairness landscape. False positive rates shift by domain, language, source credibility, and demographic group. The right approach is continuous measurement — a fairness dashboard that lives next to the accuracy dashboard and gets reviewed at every iteration.

9.0.5 5. Communication multiplies every insight

An 86% accurate model sitting in a notebook helps nobody. This book exists because I believe that clearly explaining why something works — not just proving that it works — is what transforms research into something other people can use, extend, and improve on.

9.1 Where This System Still Falls Short

Being honest about limitations is the most important credibility signal a researcher can offer. Here’s where the system genuinely struggles:

Cross-domain transfer. I trained on political news (PolitiFact) and entertainment (GossipCop). Apply this to health misinformation, financial fraud, or scientific disinformation and accuracy likely drops to 50–70%. Different domains have different linguistic fingerprints, and the model doesn’t know what it doesn’t know.

Visual and multimodal misinformation. The system sees text. Deepfakes, manipulated images, misleading infographics — this entire category is invisible to every model in this book. That’s a separate, harder problem that requires vision + text + reasoning.

Latent falsehoods. The system catches explicit false claims reasonably well. It misses subtler manipulation: true facts presented out of context, correct statistics with misleading frames, real quotes re-attributed to wrong speakers. These require world knowledge, fact-checking infrastructure, and reasoning the current system doesn’t have.

Adversarial robustness. If someone knows this model exists and deliberately writes around it — synonyms, paraphrasing, obfuscation — it will fail. This is an open research problem, and I make no claim to have solved it.

9.2 What Comes Next

9.2.1 For the research

The most important extensions are:

  • Multimodal detection: image + audio + text together, which is where most modern disinformation already lives
  • Cross-lingual transfer: does a model trained on English detect Arabic, Hindi, or Nepali misinformation? The answer is probably not, and that’s a serious equity problem
  • Longitudinal dynamics: susceptibility isn’t static. Does the same person become more or less vulnerable over time? The behavioral study was cross-sectional; a longitudinal design would be far more powerful
  • Intervention evaluation: the SEIR model shows what could work; the harder question is running real experiments on platforms to find out what actually does

9.2.2 For builders

If you’re implementing something similar:

  • Instrument your pipeline for fairness from day one — measure false positive rate by domain, source, and demographic group
  • Build the explanation layer alongside the model, not after
  • Version your models and your predictions — you’ll need to audit decisions that were made months ago
  • Design an appeal process; if your system can flag content, creators need a way to contest that flag

9.2.3 For policy

  • Audit standards: what should be measured, by whom, and how often?
  • Transparency requirements: what must platforms disclose about their detection systems?
  • Liability: who is responsible when the model makes a consequential error?
  • International coordination: misinformation doesn’t respect borders; detection systems that work in one country often fail in another

9.3 The Lessons That Actually Stuck

Rigor compounds. The investment in careful data cleaning, reliable measurement, and reproducible code paid off every time I revisited the work six months later. Shortcuts in the foundation cost three times as much downstream.

Reproducibility is a letter to your future self. Six months from now I won’t remember why I chose a particular regularization parameter. The code annotation and this book are the explanation that lets me — and anyone else — pick this up without starting from scratch.

Communicate to multiple audiences. The same finding needs different frames for an academic, a platform engineer, and a policymaker. Learning to translate across those frames is its own skill, and it matters as much as the technical work.

Ethics is ongoing. It’s not a checkbox that gets completed and filed. Every design decision — what data to collect, which errors are acceptable, what counts as explainability — has ethical weight. The only honest position is continuous scrutiny, not a one-time audit.

The best insights came from across disciplines. My clearest thinking happened in conversations with psychologists, journalists, and platform safety researchers — not data scientists. The people who understand the human problem, the content problem, and the deployment problem respectively see different parts of the system. Alone, I only see one.

9.4 A Personal Note

This project taught me something I didn’t expect: the technical and the human are not separate problems. The reason behavioral features improve accuracy isn’t because they’re clever features — it’s because misinformation is designed, by humans, to exploit other humans. Understanding that design requires taking psychology seriously, not just feature-engineering it.

I came into this work thinking about classifiers. I’m leaving it thinking about trust — how it’s built, how it’s broken, and what it actually takes to earn it.

If there’s a through-line in everything I built here, it’s this: good systems are honest about what they can’t do. The 86.1% model is useful because I know where the other 13.9% goes wrong. The SEIR simulation is useful because I know which assumptions it’s making. The behavioral profiler is useful because it comes with a disclaimer about what susceptibility scores actually mean.

Honesty about limitations isn’t weakness. It’s the foundation that makes everything else trustworthy.

9.5 The Frontier

Misinformation is a systems problem, not a classifier problem.

The classifier is one tool among many:

  • Design literacy — teach people to think critically before they share
  • Platform affordances — design for truth, not just engagement
  • Community norms — trust networks shape what travels
  • Institutional responsibility — platforms, policy, media each carry a share

The next challenge is integration: bringing behavioral science, ML, platform design, policy, and ethics together into systems that actually reduce harm without creating new kinds of it.

That’s the frontier. And there’s a lot of frontier to cover.
